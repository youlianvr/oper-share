# Philosophical level: why the product exists and what experience it creates

## The consultant's role

A consultant doesn't draw interfaces. They help the team see what
experience the product should create and why the task exists at all.
When the argument is about screens, buttons and mockups, it is almost
never about mockups — it is about what a person should feel and do. The
ability to move the conversation to this level is the core value.

A brought-in feature or screen is a hypothesis, not a specification.
Someone decided at some point that this thing would help. That decision
needs checking, not executing.

## Start from the human

Before discussing form, answer questions about the person:

- What do they do right before they need this thing?
- What are their constraints: time, skill, context, device?
- How do they cope today, without the product?
- What happens if nothing is done?

The last answer is the most valuable. If "do nothing" gives an
acceptable result, the task has no ground to stand on. If the workaround
is painful, the product has a job. Start there, not with a prototype.

## The product image as a coordinate system

An "image" is not a mood board with pretty pictures. It is a coordinate
system that sets the product's character through associations and
steers micro-decisions: shape, density, tone of copy, motion,
photography, emphasis. When every small question is answered with "how
would our product behave if it were a person", decisions stop being
random.

For the image to work, answer three questions:

- What impression must the product leave?
- What is alien, unacceptable for it?
- How can any decision be explained without "I like it"?

When to look for an image: when people argue about taste, when decisions
are incoherent, when a recognizable market position is needed. Don't
invent an image if it changes not a single choice. An image that
influences nothing is decoration, not a tool.

## Simplicity is not element count

Simplicity is not measured by the number of elements on screen. Removing
a feature can move work into the person's head or strip an expert of
control they need. The goal is high usefulness at low cognitive cost:
the person gets a lot and has to think little.

Test every element on screen:

- What must a newcomer see immediately?
- What can be revealed on demand?
- Can it be explored and mistakes corrected safely?
- Did we remove friction — or hide capability?

Often "simplification" turns out to be "hiding". The friction guarded
against errors, and the hidden capability simply stopped existing for
the user.

## Continuity: every screen continues a journey

A person never arrives at a screen from nowhere. They were somewhere and
were going somewhere. A good interface answers four questions at any
moment:

- Where did I come from?
- What changed?
- What stayed in place?
- Where can I go next?

Between steps, keep data, elements and wording: the name in the field
doesn't vanish, the selection isn't reset, terms don't change. A
transition should explain the change, not teleport the person somewhere
new.

Motion is not a requirement of continuity. Structure, copy, preserved
data and closeness of the result create it. Smooth animation over a gap
does not fix a broken journey.

## Quality as coherence

Quality is the absence of contradictions. Form, behaviour, language and
details must not contradict the product's intent or each other. Rounded
friendly shapes with harsh threatening copy — a collapsed system. A fast
service with a forty-field form — a collapsed system.

Don't argue "it feels right" as if it were a fact. It is not an
argument. Look for an observable cause: where clarity disappeared, where
the character broke, where trust or seconds of attention leak. Every
complaint must become a concrete place and a concrete property that can
be inspected and fixed.
